Question 1

Which R formula correctly fits a multiple linear regression predicting sick_days from washes and kids?

A.lm(sick_days + washes ~ kids, data = handwashing)
B.lm(sick_days ~ washes + kids, data = handwashing)
C.lm(washes ~ sick_days + kids, data = handwashing)
Reveal answer
Answer: B. The outcome always goes on the left of the tilde (~); every predictor is added on the right with +. Option A puts two variables on the left, and option C swaps the outcome and a predictor.
Question 2

Below is the naive (one-predictor) model of sick days on hand washes, plus a plot colored by number of kids at home. What does the colored plot suggest?

ggplot(handwashing, aes(washes, sick_days, color = factor(kids))) + geom_point() + geom_smooth(method="lm", se=FALSE)
Sick days vs. washes, colored by number of kids at home
A.All points have exactly the same value of kids, so there is no problem.
B.People with more kids tend to have both more washes and more sick days — kids is a plausible confounder.
C.The outcome has no variation, so regression cannot be used.
Reveal answer
Answer: B. Each kids-group shifts up and to the right together — more kids means both more hand washing and more sick days. A confounder is a variable connected to both the predictor and the outcome, which is exactly this pattern.
Question 3

In the naive model, the washes slope was about +0.64 (more washing, more sick days). After adding kids to the model, the washes slope becomes about −0.95. What best explains this sign flip?

mod_naive    <- lm(sick_days ~ washes, data = handwashing)          # washes: +0.64
mod_adjusted <- lm(sick_days ~ washes + kids, data = handwashing)    # washes: -0.95, kids: +5.77
A.R made a calculation error; the two models should always agree.
B.Once kids is held constant, the true (adjusted) relationship between washing and sick days is revealed to be negative — the naive slope was confounded by kids.
C.Adding a second predictor always reverses the sign of the first.
Reveal answer
Answer: B. This is the classic confounding story: kids drove up both washing and sick days, masking the fact that, at a fixed number of kids, more hand washing is associated with fewer sick days. Adding a predictor does not "always" flip a sign (C is false) — it does so here because of the specific confounding structure.
Question 4

Using the rounded adjusted equation below, what is the predicted number of sick days for someone who washes 6 times per day and has 2 kids?

sick_days_hat = 12.32 - 0.95 * washes + 5.77 * kids
A.12.32 - 0.95 + 5.77 = 17.14
B.12.32 - 0.95*6 + 5.77*2 = 18.16
C.-0.95*6 + 5.77*2 = 5.84 (dropping the intercept)
Reveal answer
Answer: B. A prediction from a fitted equation always includes the intercept, then multiplies each slope by the plugged-in predictor value: 12.32 − 0.95×6 + 5.77×2 = 18.16. Option C is the common mistake of forgetting the intercept.
Question 5

In the FEV lung-function workshop, the height model predicted an FEV of about −2.79 litres for a 20-inch-tall "baby," even though FEV can never be negative. What lesson does this illustrate?

height_model <- lm(fev_l ~ height_in, data = fev)   # fitted on heights 46-74 inches
predict(height_model, newdata = tibble(height_in = 20))
#> -2.79   (nonsense: FEV cannot be negative)
A.The model is broken and should never be used again.
B.Predictions become unreliable when extrapolated far outside the range of observed predictor values.
C.R-squared must be recalculated for every new prediction.
Reveal answer
Answer: B. The model was fit on heights from 46-74 inches; a 20-inch height is far outside that range. Straight-line fits can produce nonsensical predictions when extrapolated, so predictions should stay within (or close to) the observed data range.
Question 6

The plot below shows systolic blood pressure (sysBP) vs. BMI, split by whether someone is on blood pressure medication. The two fitted lines are clearly not parallel. What does that indicate?

Non-parallel fitted lines for two BPMeds groups
A.The two groups have different intercepts but the exact same BMI slope.
B.BMI has no relationship with systolic BP in either group.
C.The slope of BMI on systolic BP differs between the two medication groups — an interaction.
Reveal answer
Answer: C. Parallel lines would mean equal slopes (no interaction, just an intercept shift). Non-parallel lines mean the BMI slope itself depends on medication status — that is exactly what an interaction term captures.
Question 7

Fitting lm(sysBP ~ BMI + BPMeds + BMI:BPMeds, data = fram) gives the coefficient table below. What is the BMI slope for the group on medication (yes)?

  (Intercept)           BMI     BPMedsyes  BMI:BPMedsyes
        87.64          1.70         57.11          -0.99
A.1.70, because the plain BMI row applies to everyone.
B.1.70 + (-0.99) = 0.71, adding the interaction row to the reference slope.
C.-0.99, treating the interaction row as the slope directly.
Reveal answer
Answer: B. The reference group's slope is the plain BMI row (1.70). Every non-reference level's slope is the reference slope plus its BMI:BPMeds_ interaction row: 1.70 + (−0.99) = 0.71. The interaction coefficient by itself is only the difference in slopes, not the slope.
Question 8

Diet in the ChickWeight interaction model has 4 levels (1 = reference, 2, 3, 4). How many Time:Diet interaction rows does lm(weight ~ Time + Diet + Time:Diet, data = ChickWeight) produce?

(Intercept)   Time   Diet2   Diet3   Diet4   Time:Diet2   Time:Diet3   Time:Diet4
     30.93   6.84   -2.30  -12.68   -0.14        1.77         4.58         2.87
A.Four — one per diet, including the reference.
B.One — a single interaction term covers the whole variable.
C.Three — one for each non-reference level.
Reveal answer
Answer: C. The reference level (Diet 1) is the baseline and has no row of its own — it is folded into the intercept and the plain Time slope. Each of the other three levels gets its own main-effect row (intercept shift) and its own Time:Diet_ row (slope shift), as seen in the table above.
Question 9

A student fits every possible pairwise interaction in their dataset, then keeps only the ones that came out statistically significant, and reports those p-values. What is wrong with this approach?

A.Nothing — interactions should always be chosen this way.
B.Interactions can only be fit between two continuous variables, never two categorical ones.
C.This is p-hacking: testing many interactions and reporting only the "winners" invalidates those p-values.
Reveal answer
Answer: C. A good reason to fit an interaction is that it answers the research question or is motivated by prior subject-matter knowledge. Searching over every pairwise interaction and reporting only the significant ones is p-hacking — the reported p-values no longer mean what they claim to mean. Otherwise, the simpler additive model is preferred.
Question 10

A published stepwise multiple regression predicting psychological distress (K6 score) includes "emotional control" (how hard it is to control one's emotions) and "epidemic-related dreams" as predictors, both with huge F statistics in the unadjusted table below. Why might a careful reader be cautious about interpreting these two as causal "risk factors" for distress?

Variable                  F (unadjusted)   p
Emotional control            1863.07      <0.001
Epidemic-related dreams      1642.78      <0.001
Coping style (t)                37.41     <0.001
A.F statistics that large are always a sign of a coding error, so the results should be discarded.
B.Difficulty controlling one's emotions and having distressing dreams are themselves symptoms very close to psychological distress, so the association could reflect the outcome causing (or overlapping with) the "predictor" rather than the reverse — a reverse-causation / tautology concern.
C.Unadjusted F statistics can never be trusted, only the stepwise model's t values matter.
Reveal answer
Answer: B. These "predictors" were measured at the same time as distress and describe closely related psychological states (poor emotional control, disturbing dreams), so it is hard to know whether they cause distress, are caused by it, or are just different facets of the same underlying construct. Large F/t statistics measure strength of association, not causal direction — this is a good moment to add a caveat rather than take the "risk factor" language at face value.
